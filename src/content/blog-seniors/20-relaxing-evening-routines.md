**Quick answer:** A calming evening routine for older adults works best when it replaces screen time with something quiet and hands-on — a puzzle page, a few minutes of drawing, or a simple word game — in the hour or so before bed. This isn't a sleep treatment, and it won't fix insomnia on its own. It's a small, low-effort swap that fits naturally alongside the general evening habits that sleep researchers already recommend, like keeping a consistent bedtime and keeping screens out of the bedroom.

Below is a simple, hour-by-hour way to build that swap into an evening, plus what the National Institute on Aging actually recommends for better sleep.

## Why the hour before bed matters

Light from phones, tablets, computers and TVs can delay the body's natural signal that it's time to sleep, and shows or scrolling that are upsetting or overstimulating can keep the mind active right when it should be winding down. None of that is specific to older adults — it's general sleep-hygiene guidance that applies at any age. What changes the equation for a wind-down routine is simply having something calm and screen-free to do instead, so "no screens" doesn't mean "nothing to do."

## A simple hour-by-hour evening routine

**One hour before bed — finish up, dim the lights.** Wrap up anything active for the day. Dim overhead lighting and switch to a lamp if you have one; dimmer, warmer light in the evening supports the body's natural wind-down.

**45 minutes before bed — put the screens away.** Move phones, tablets and the TV remote out of easy reach. This is the point where a quiet activity takes over.

**30 minutes before bed — one calm, hands-on activity.** This is the heart of the routine: a large-print word search, a few pages of gentle tracing or drawing, or a true-or-false trivia page. Keep it short and low-pressure — the goal is calm engagement, not finishing something.

**15 minutes before bed — put the activity away and settle in.** Set the puzzle or sketchbook aside, get into bed or your usual resting spot, and let the room settle into quiet.

**At bedtime — same time, every night.** Going to bed and waking up at roughly the same time each day, including weekends, is one of the simplest and most consistently recommended habits for better sleep at any age.

## What sleep guidance actually says

The National Institute on Aging's general guidance for older adults includes several habits worth knowing, even though this routine isn't a treatment for sleep problems:

- **Keep a consistent schedule.** Going to bed and waking up at the same time every day, including weekends and while traveling, helps the body settle into a rhythm.
- **Skip late naps.** A nap late in the afternoon or evening can make it harder to fall asleep at night.
- **Keep screens out of the bedroom.** Light from TVs, computers, phones and tablets can delay sleep, and upsetting or exciting content can keep the mind too active to wind down.
- **Watch caffeine and alcohol later in the day.** Caffeine — in coffee, tea, soda and chocolate — and alcohol can both interfere with sleep, even in small amounts, especially later in the day.
- **If you can't fall asleep, don't lie there fighting it.** The general advice is to get up after about 20 minutes of being unable to sleep, do something calm in dim light, and go back to bed once you feel sleepy again.

A quiet puzzle or drawing activity fits naturally into several of these habits at once: it replaces screen time, it's calming rather than stimulating, and it gives the mind something gentle to settle into instead of the day's leftover thoughts.

## What this routine is not

This is a wind-down ritual, not a treatment for insomnia or any sleep disorder. It won't fix a sleep problem that has an underlying medical cause, and persistent trouble sleeping is worth discussing with a doctor. What a calm, screen-free evening activity *can* do is give the hour before bed a gentler shape — something to look forward to instead of another stretch of scrolling.

## Frequently asked questions

**Will a quiet puzzle before bed actually improve my sleep?**
This routine is a calming wind-down ritual, not a proven sleep treatment, and it isn't a substitute for the general sleep habits recommended by the National Institute on Aging, such as a consistent schedule and keeping screens out of the bedroom. What it can do is give you a screen-free, low-pressure way to spend the hour before bed.

**Why avoid screens specifically in the evening?**
Light from phones, tablets, computers and TVs can delay the body's natural wind-down signal, and overstimulating content can keep the mind active right when it should be settling.

**What if I can't fall asleep even with a calm routine?**
General guidance is to avoid lying in bed fighting it — get up after about 20 minutes, do something calm in dim light, and return to bed once you feel sleepy. Ongoing sleep trouble is worth raising with a doctor.

**Does caffeine timing really matter this much?**
Yes — caffeine from coffee, tea, soda or chocolate can interfere with sleep even when consumed earlier in the day, and it's worth watching more closely later in the afternoon and evening.

**What's a good quiet activity if I don't enjoy puzzles?**
Gentle drawing or tracing is a good alternative. The specific activity matters less than keeping it calm, screen-free, and free of any pressure to finish.

## Key takeaways

- The hour before bed matters most — **replace screens**, don't just remove them.
- A simple hour-by-hour routine: **dim lights, put screens away, one calm activity, settle in, consistent bedtime.**
- NIA's general sleep guidance also covers **naps, caffeine, alcohol, and what to do if you can't fall asleep.**
- This is a **wind-down ritual, not a sleep treatment** — persistent sleep trouble is worth discussing with a doctor.
